# US-English house style

Consult this reference for localization and editorial choices. Chicago informs the conventions below. Sentence-case headings, flexible sentence lengths, and final-only output are EN-US house choices.

## Spelling and vocabulary

Use American forms in ordinary prose:

- color, behavior, favorite
- center, theater, meter
- organize, recognize, analyze
- traveled, canceled, labeled
- license as both noun and verb; practice as both noun and verb

Preserve official names such as "World Health Organization" and "Centre for Science and Policy" exactly. Preserve quoted spelling and identifiers such as `colour_mode`.

Choose vocabulary that the intended reader knows. "Elevator" and "cell phone" suit a US audience. Keep a British term when it names a specifically British thing or belongs to a quotation.

Distinguish forms by function: "log in" is a verb; "login" is a noun or modifier. Follow exact product labels even when their spelling differs.

## Punctuation

- Use the serial comma: "Save the file, restart the service, and check the log."
- Use double quotation marks, then single marks for a quotation within a quotation.
- Put commas and periods inside closing quotation marks in ordinary prose. Put colons and semicolons outside. Place question marks and exclamation points according to what they apply to.
- Use backticks for exact strings, commands, paths, and identifiers. Surrounding punctuation stays outside: Enter `ready`, then press Enter.
- Keep straight or curly quotation marks consistent with the medium. Straight quotes suit code-oriented text. Curly quotes are appropriate in typeset prose and are not evidence of AI writing.

Prefer a period when clauses can stand as separate sentences. Keep a semicolon when it usefully connects closely related clauses or separates complex list items. Use a dash for an intentional interruption, not as the default connection between ideas.

Avoid making a grammatical sentence choppy just to remove punctuation. Preserve an author's deliberate punctuation when matching a sample.

## Hyphens and compounds

Hyphenate a compound modifier before a noun when needed for clarity: "a well-tested change." Usually leave it open after the noun: "the change is well tested."

Do not add a hyphen after an adverb ending in "-ly": "a carefully written report." Keep established dictionary spellings and technical terms. Resolve doubtful compounds by meaning and common usage rather than applying a blanket rule.

## Headings and titles

Use sentence case for ordinary headings: "Configure the client." Capitalize proper names and product names correctly.

Preserve the wording and capitalization of published titles unless the user requests editorial restyling. Apply Chicago title case when the task requires it. Sentence case is this skill's default for headings, not a universal Chicago requirement.

Use headings to locate information. Avoid title-plus-subtitle constructions that repeat the same point and bold labels on every short bullet.

## Numbers and measurements

- Use commas for thousands and a period for decimals in localized prose: "1,500.25."
- In general prose, spell out zero through nine as a house default. Use numerals for technical quantities, comparisons, versions, dates, money, and measurements. Follow a requested publication style instead.
- Preserve numeric values, signs, ranges, significant digits, and stated precision. Do not change "up to 10" to "10" or "about 30" to "30."
- Preserve currencies and units. A US-English rewrite can still say "£1,500.50," "10 kg," or "20 °C." Use currency codes when the source identifies a currency and the symbol would be ambiguous.
- Use a percent sign in technical and data-heavy text, such as "5%." Follow the requested prose style elsewhere. Preserve the difference between percent and percentage points.

The small-number rule is an EN-US house choice. Chicago's general prose rule spells out more numbers and also allows alternatives for different kinds of material.

## Dates and times

Prefer "September 28, 2026" in prose. In a sentence, use the second comma: "On September 28, 2026, the service launched."

Preserve ISO dates such as `2026-09-28` in technical material. Avoid introducing ambiguous forms such as "04/05/2026." If the source date is ambiguous and context does not resolve it, ask whether it means April 5 or May 4.

Use "2:30 p.m." in ordinary prose when appropriate, or retain a useful 24-hour format. Preserve the time zone and the actual time. Do not infer a date, daylight-saving offset, or currency from the target language.

## Grammar and tone

Use complete sentences in prose and imperative verbs in instructions. Short UI labels and headings can be fragments.

Contractions such as "it's," "don't," and "you'll" are natural in chat and many product documents. Spell them out when emphasis, ambiguity, or the requested formal register calls for it.

Use singular "they" when appropriate. A split infinitive, sentence-ending preposition, or sentence beginning with "and" or "but" is not inherently an error. Choose the construction that reads clearly.
